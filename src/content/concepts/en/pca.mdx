**PCA** (Principal Component Analysis) transforms a set of correlated variables into a smaller number of uncorrelated variables, called **principal components**, that retain most of the variance.

## What it is for

1. **Reducing noise**: by keeping the components that explain most of the variance, we discard directions dominated by noise (like the red axis PC3).
2. **Visualizing**: it lets you project high-dimensional data down to 2 or 3 dimensions for plotting.
3. **Gaining efficiency**: models train faster with fewer variables.

## Geometric intuition

The idea is to "rotate" the data space until we look at it from the most informative angle: the one that maximizes the spread of the points.

## The algorithm step by step

### 1. Centering and standardization

So that variables with large scales (like "income") do not dominate those with small scales (like "age"), we rescale each column:

$$
Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

This leaves each variable with **zero mean** and **unit variance**. If you only center (without dividing by $\sigma$), you work with the original covariance matrix; if you standardize, with the correlation matrix.

### 2. Covariance matrix

We compute the matrix $\Sigma$, which summarizes the linear relationships between all pairs of variables:

$$
\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} Z^\top Z
$$

### 3. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The principal axes are the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$. They are obtained by solving the **characteristic equation**:

$$
\det(\Sigma - \lambda I) = 0
$$

- **Eigenvalues** $\lambda$: indicate **importance**; each one is the variance captured along its direction.
- **Eigenvectors** $v$: indicate **direction**; they define the new axes (the lines in the simulator).

The fraction of variance explained by component $j$ is $\lambda_j / \sum_k \lambda_k$.

### 4. Projection

We choose the $k$ eigenvectors with the largest eigenvalues, collect them as columns of $W_k$, and project:

$$
T = Z \, W_k
$$

$T$ holds the reduced data, ready for plotting or for training a model.

## Caution

<Callout type="warning">
PCA is linear and sensitive to scale: without standardizing, the variable with the largest magnitude dominates PC1. Also, "more variance" does not always mean "more useful for prediction".
</Callout>
