**Pearson correlation** is probably the most widely used measure of the relationship between two variables. Unlike [covariance](/en/concepts/covarianza), it is normalized and always lies between **-1 and 1**.

## Definition

The correlation $\rho$ divides the covariance by the product of the standard deviations:

$$
\rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \, \sigma_Y}
$$

where $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)$ is the covariance and $\sigma_X$, $\sigma_Y$ are the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$. With sample data, the estimator is written $r$.

## Interpretation

- **1**: perfect positive linear relationship.
- **0**: no linear relationship.
- **-1**: perfect negative linear relationship.

## Play with a sample

Change the target correlation and the sample size. You will see that the observed coefficient fluctuates because of sampling noise, even though the underlying relationship is always the same.

With few observations, $r$ can be far from the true value. More data usually stabilizes the estimate.

## Why it is better than covariance

Because it lives in a fixed range, it lets you compare the relationship between "height and weight" with that between "temperature and electricity consumption" without worrying about units.

## Caution

<Callout type="warning">
**Correlation does not imply causation.** Two variables moving together does not mean one causes the other: there may be a hidden third variable (a confounder) or just a coincidence. Also, Pearson only detects **linear** relationships: a U-shaped relationship can have a correlation close to 0.
</Callout>
