**Naive Bayes** uses Bayes' theorem to estimate which class is most likely given the observed evidence. Its "naive" assumption is that the variables are conditionally independent within each class.

## The decision rule

Bayes' theorem gives the posterior probability of each class $C$ given the attributes $X = (x_1, \dots, x_m)$:

$$
P(C \mid X) = \frac{P(X \mid C)\,P(C)}{P(X)}
$$

The "naive" assumption factorizes the likelihood as a product of simple terms, one per variable:

$$
P(X \mid C) = \prod_{j=1}^{m} P(x_j \mid C)
$$

Since $P(X)$ is the same for all classes, it is enough to compare the numerators and pick the largest:

$$
\hat{C} = \arg\max_{C} \; P(C) \prod_{j=1}^{m} P(x_j \mid C)
$$

In practice, logarithms are summed instead of multiplying probabilities, to avoid numerical underflow.

## What to look for

- With only a few counts you already get a useful posterior probability.
- The independence assumption is strong, but it often works well, especially for ranking classes.
- Its probabilities tend to be poorly calibrated: it gets the class right but overstates its confidence.
- It is especially competitive on text and on high-dimensional problems.

## Applications

Spam filtering, sentiment analysis, document classification, and tasks where training and prediction speed matter a lot.
