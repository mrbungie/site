**Logistic regression** models the probability that the positive class occurs. It starts from a linear combination of the input variables, but squeezes the output between **0 and 1** using a sigmoid function.

## The fundamental equation

The estimated probability of belonging to the positive class is

$$
P(y=1 \mid x) = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)}}
$$

Instead of predicting an unbounded line, the model transforms the linear combination into an **S**-shaped curve.

## From the logit to classification

Solving for the linear combination gives the **logit** (log of the odds):

$$
\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x
$$

The parameters can be read from it:

- $\beta_0$ shifts the curve to the left or right.
- $\beta_1$ controls how abrupt the transition between classes is; also, $e^{\beta_1}$ is the factor by which the odds are multiplied when $x$ increases by one unit.
- The **threshold** $t$ turns the probability into a decision: if $p \ge t$, the model predicts class 1. With $t = 0.5$ the boundary is at $x = -\beta_0/\beta_1$.

## How it is fitted

The coefficients are estimated by maximizing the likelihood, which is equivalent to minimizing the **log loss**:

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i) \Big]
$$

## What to look for

- If you raise the threshold, the model becomes more conservative when labeling positives.
- If you increase the slope, the transition between classes becomes sharper.
- **Accuracy** can improve or worsen depending on the balance between false positives and false negatives.
- **Log loss** penalizes confident but wrong predictions more heavily.

## Applications

Fraud detection, customer churn prediction, disease risk estimation and, in general, any problem with a **yes/no**, **success/failure** or **buy/no buy** response.
