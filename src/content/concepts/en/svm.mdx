The **Support Vector Machine** looks for a decision boundary that not only separates the classes, but does so leaving the widest possible **margin** between them.

## The mathematical idea

In its linear version, SVM defines a separating hyperplane:

$$
w^\top x + b = 0
$$

A point is classified according to the sign of $w^\top x + b$. The **margin** (the distance between the two classes on either side of the boundary) equals $2/\lVert w \rVert$, so maximizing the margin is equivalent to minimizing $\lVert w \rVert$.

When the classes are not perfectly separable, some error is allowed through the **hinge loss** and a regularization parameter $C$:

$$
\min_{w,b} \; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{n} \max\bigl(0,\; 1 - y_i (w^\top x_i + b)\bigr)
$$

A large $C$ punishes errors and gives narrow margins; a small $C$ tolerates errors and gives wide margins.

## What to look for

- The **support vectors** are the most critical points: if they move, the boundary changes; the rest do not affect it.
- A kernel such as **RBF** allows curved boundaries when a straight line is not enough.
- A wide margin makes the model more robust to small perturbations.

## Applications

SVM is used in text classification, bioinformatics, computer vision and problems where good geometric separation matters more than a calibrated probability.
