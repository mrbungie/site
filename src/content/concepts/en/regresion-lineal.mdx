**Linear regression** looks for the line that best fits a set of points. It is the foundation of much of modern machine learning and the standard tool for predicting numerical values.

## The model

With one explanatory variable $x$, the model is

$$
y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon
$$

where $\beta_0$ is the intercept (the expected value of $y$ when $x=0$), $\beta_1$ is the slope and $\varepsilon$ is the random error.

OLS chooses the coefficients that minimize the **sum of squared errors** (SSE):

$$
\mathrm{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
$$

In the simple case there is a closed-form solution, and it connects with the earlier concepts:

$$
\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(X,Y)}{\operatorname{var}(X)} = \rho_{X,Y}\,\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X},
\qquad
\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}
$$

## How well it fits

The coefficient of determination $R^2$ measures the fraction of the variance of $y$ that the model explains:

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}}, \qquad \mathrm{SST} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2
$$

In simple regression, $R^2 = \rho^2$. A high $R^2$ does not guarantee that the model is correct: always check the residuals.

## Applications

From predicting the price of a house from its square footage to estimating the effect of a marketing campaign on sales.
